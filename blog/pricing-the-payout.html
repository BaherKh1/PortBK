Pricing the payout, not the view

14 October 2026 · 4 min read

The first version of Relay's rate card was a flat fee per video. A brand paid €400, a creator made the clip, and everyone moved on. It was simple, easy to explain and wrong in a way that took two campaigns to notice: the brands with the smallest budgets got the most careful creators, and the brands with real reach targets got whatever was left.

We changed to a price per thousand delivered views. The brand sets a budget cap, the creator sets a rate, and the payout is calculated from verified views after the campaign closes. The brand knows its worst case. The creator knows the upside. The platform is not in the middle guessing what a view is worth.

Why "delivered" and not "posted"

Paying on posting rewards volume. Paying on delivered views rewards work that people actually watch. The difference is not philosophical; it is the entire incentive. A creator who posts eight rushed clips earns less than one who posts two that land. That is the behaviour we wanted, and the rate card had to say so out loud.

The word "delivered" also forced us to define a view. Ours is a view that passes the platform's own threshold and survives the first 48 hours of filtering. It is not perfect. It is consistent, written down, and visible to both sides before anyone signs.

The first rate card failed the trust test

We launched with a single rate for every category. A beauty creator and a finance creator were offered the same number, even though their audiences cost wildly different amounts to reach. The creators noticed before the brands did. Two of our best people stopped applying, and one wrote back a sentence I still keep: "You are pricing the video, not the audience."

So we split the rate card by category and by market. More columns, more arguments, better outcomes. A campaign in Portugal no longer subsidises one in Germany, and a niche audience is no longer valued like a general one.

What we kept simple on purpose

  • One number for the brand. A single cost per thousand views, all fees included.
  • One number for the creator. Their rate, paid in full, on a published schedule.
  • A cap before launch. No brand can spend past its budget because a clip unexpectedly travels.
  • A dispute window. Seven days to question a view count, with a human on the other end.

Everything else we automate. The invoice, the payout batch and the reconciliation run as scheduled jobs. The rate card is a document a person can read, not a model that decides in private.

The lesson I would repeat

Price the thing you want more of. We wanted clips people finish, so we paid for views that count. If we had paid for posts, we would have built a content mill with better branding. The rate card is the product strategy written in numbers, and creators read it faster than they read the landing page.
